“Does this spoon stick to a magnet?” is an easy question to ask when checking stainless-steel cutlery. It is not a reliable answer to “What exact grade did we buy?” Magnetic response varies with stainless-steel family and processing. In particular, cold working can give some austenitic stainless steel a partial magnetic response. The [British Stainless Steel Association's guide to on-site grade sorting](https://bssa.org.uk/bssa_articles/2-on-site-methods-for-stainless-steel-grade-product-sorting/) says conclusive grade identification calls for calibrated chemical analysis; its [magnetic-permeability note](https://bssa.org.uk/bssa_articles/effect-of-cold-work-and-heat-treatment-on-the-magnetic-permeability-of-austenitic-stainless-steels/) explains the effect of working on austenitic steel.

For a cutlery order, the useful question is therefore: **Which grade was agreed for each component, and how will the supplied batch be linked to that specification?** A knife, fork and spoon need not have an identical component specification. Record the agreement rather than assuming one grade from the set name or a quick magnetic check.

## A component-level material check

| Record | Buyer asks for | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Component list | Separate knife, dinner fork, dinner spoon and other pieces | Prevents one grade statement being read as applying to all pieces |
| Agreed grade | Grade designation for each component in the quotation and order | Establishes what is actually being purchased |
| Sample identity | Model, sample revision and marked component | Connects approval to the offered configuration |
| Batch link | Lot or production reference and a traceable document | Connects delivered goods to records |
| Verification method | Qualified chemical analysis where exact grade confirmation is required | A magnet alone cannot give an exact composition |
| Acceptance owner | Who compares evidence with the order before release | Prevents an observation being mistaken for approval |

Yumingxing's [cutlery collection](https://www.mteng.ltd/en/stainless-steel-cutlery/) advises confirming material grade by component, and its [cutlery RFQ guide](/en/blog/what-to-include-in-a-cutlery-rfq/) asks buyers to specify the grade and supporting requirements clearly. Those pages do not disclose the grade of an unconfirmed sample or certify a particular batch.

A magnetic check can be recorded as a field observation when comparing parts or spotting an unexpected change. Describe the component, location and conditions; do not translate “magnetic” into a precise grade, food-contact approval or corrosion-resistance promise. If the agreed grade matters to acceptance, ask the supplier for the relevant documents and arrange suitable qualified analysis under the purchase agreement.

Before placing a bulk order, put the component table in the RFQ and state which document, sampling plan and test method will close any grade question. That turns a quick magnet demonstration into a controlled procurement decision without claiming more than the observation can show.
